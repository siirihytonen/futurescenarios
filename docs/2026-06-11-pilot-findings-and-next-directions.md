# Pilot findings and next directions

Notes for supervision. One pilot run of the AI cooling-allocation study (agency x community,
four prototypes, within-subjects, ratings plus a focus-group map and discussion). The aim here is
to record what the pilot showed and to frame the design decisions worth tightening together.

## What we ran

One pilot session, about 1.5 hours. The participant(s) used all four prototypes (C1 high
agency + high community, C2 high + private, C3 low + shared, C4 low + private), gave per-cell
ratings, then placed the four on a map and discussed them.

## What the pilot showed

**The unfavourable outcome landed (H1).** Everyone felt the prototypes constrained them in some
way, across all four versions. The premise holds: the result reads as something done to the user,
not a benefit.

**The acceptance pattern matched the prediction.** Least accepted was the low-agency, private
version (C4, the floor). Most liked was the high-agency, high-community version (C1). This is the
direction H3 and H4 predict: both levers move acceptance, and the shared, high-control combination
sits at the top.

**Community changed how people reasoned about acceptance.** The communal framing made participants
reflect on whether they accepted the decision, which is the buffering signal the study is built
around.

**The strongest qualitative finding: when people will let the AI decide.** Asked when they would
accept the AI deciding for them, participants named two conditions:
1. Tasks that are heavily computational, where a lot of data has to be weighed. This lines up with
   Esch et al. on mechanical tasks raising acceptance of AI.
2. When there is an already-agreed plan, like the communal one, they would hand the decision over,
   on the condition that the process is very transparent.
   This points to a tight contribution: acceptable delegation under an unfavourable shared
   allocation rests on computability, collective legitimacy, and transparency. That is more
   specific than "people accept AI when it helps them," and it is the part most worth sharpening.

**The method worked (RQ3).** Participants mapped all four versions correctly and found them
distinct enough to tell apart. The four-world mapping approach discriminates. Open question: if it
works here, would it also work with simpler prototypes?

**Sustainability reflection was hard to elicit.** Getting people to reflect on their own
consumption or sustainability behaviour through an imagined prototype did not really happen. This
is a weak spot for RQ2b / H6 and is worth a decision (see below).

## Methodological problems observed

- **Length.** About 1.5 hours is too long.
- **Learning and carryover.** Because the system was the same across versions, people understood it
  better as they went. Good for comprehension, but it is a confound that needs counterbalancing.
- **Forgetting in the reflection phase.** By the discussion, people had trouble recalling which
  version was which, though they could still point to specific features (the communal grid in
  particular).
- **Comprehension varied.** Two participants found the concept easy; one struggled. The scenarios
  need to be clearer, and the situation ("why the prototypes are shaped the way they are") needs to
  be justified to the user.
- **The quantitative scales added little at this N.** They are only worth keeping if the study runs
  with enough people to support them.
- **People wanted to react immediately.** Participants wanted to talk about each prototype right
  after trying it, not only at the end.
- **Too complex.** The prototypes were more complex than they needed to be. This was deliberate (we
  wanted to test the rich versions), and the pilot confirms simpler would serve better.

## Two directions to decide between

**Direction A: breadth and measurement.** Two use cases (for example cooling plus a second
allocation domain), simpler prototypes, fewer questionnaire items, tighter research questions, more
participants. Buys some generalisability across domains and a basis for quantitative claims. Costs
depth, and it needs real recruitment to make the numbers mean anything.

**Direction B: depth and reasoning.** One use case as now, simpler prototypes, pair interviews or
small focus groups, discussion straight after each prototype, qualitative-led. Buys rich material
on acceptance, fairness, and the conditions for delegation, which is what you said you care about.
Costs generalisability and any inferential claim.

**A hybrid** is possible: one use case, simpler prototypes, keep only the manipulation checks and
the favorability item as light quant, and put the weight on the qualitative map and discussion.

Given your stated interest (acceptance, fairness, and the lived experience) and the small-N
reality of focus groups, Direction B or the hybrid fits best. Direction A is the right call only if
the contribution you want is cross-domain generalisability, which would change the thesis story and
the recruitment burden.

## Fixes to apply in either direction

- **Simplify the prototypes** while keeping them distinct. The pilot shows distinctiveness survives;
  cut features, not the contrast.
- **Frame the world harder.** Give each scenario a fuller description of the state of things in that
  near future, so people can imagine into it and play along, and explain why the versions differ.
  Keep an instruction sheet beside the participant so they can return to the "flow," and make clear
  the task is to look at the prototype and interact only a little.
- **React early, compare late.** Take a short immediate reaction after each prototype (a word and a
  gut score), then do the full comparative map and discussion at the end. This cuts forgetting
  without losing the holistic comparison. Run the map soon after exposure.
- **Counterbalance order** (Latin square) and report the learning effect rather than pretending it
  away.
- **Decide the fate of sustainability reflection.** If it will not elicit on an imagined prototype,
  either drop RQ2b / H6, reframe it as "did the collective framing make the bigger picture salient
  at all," or measure it differently. Cutting it tightens scope.
- **Trim the session toward about an hour.** Fewer items, simpler prototypes, and discuss-after-each
  all help.

## Open questions for the meeting

1. Direction A (two use cases, more quant) or B (one use case, qualitative depth), or the hybrid?
2. Keep, trim, or drop the quantitative scales?
3. Keep four cells, or reduce (for example drop to a sharper contrast) to cut length and learning?
4. Keep, reframe, or drop the sustainability-reflection question?
5. Full focus groups or pair interviews?
6. Is "the conditions under which people accept AI deciding an unfavourable shared allocation
   (computability, collective legitimacy, transparency)" the contribution we want to lead with?

## A tighter RQ framing to react to

If we lead with the delegation finding, the questions could narrow to something like: under an
unfavourable AI-mediated allocation, how do agency and community framing shape perceived acceptance
and fairness, and what conditions make people willing to hand the decision to the AI? That keeps
acceptance and fairness central, folds in the strongest pilot result, and lets the sustainability
strand drop or shrink.
