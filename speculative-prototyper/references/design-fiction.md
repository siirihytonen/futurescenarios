# Design fiction: building scenarios worth discussing

A research probe is only as good as the situation it puts the participant in. The failure mode,
documented by Ek et al. (2024) and Blythe (2023), is that AI-generated scenarios drift toward
smooth, solution-shaped worlds where the technology quietly fixes everything. Those produce
polite, flat data. Your job is the opposite: build a believable near-future that contains a
real bind.

## Pastiche, then friction (Blythe 2023)

Start from recognisable near-future tropes so the world reads as plausible without exposition:
a heatwave summer, a strained grid, a building that meters and allocates, an assistant with a
name. Pastiche gets you believability cheaply. Then do the part the model tends to skip: push
friction back in. The generator must act as a "prompt sculptor", re-inserting the mundane,
inconvenient, human texture that a probabilistic model sands off. Concretely:

- A consequence that is annoying and specific, not abstract (a hot flat at the exact hour you
  get home from an early shift; the dog alone in the heat).
- A reason the system's logic is defensible but still costs you (it protected the building, and
  that is genuinely good, and you still sweat tonight).
- A neighbour who is sympathetic, not a villain (the flat with a baby that cannot go without
  cooling).

## The contradiction is the point

Every scenario must contain at least one genuine contradiction with no clean answer. This is
what gives the focus group something to argue about and what lets the study see how people
weigh competing goods. Pick one (or build your own in the same shape):

A good contradiction test: can a smart, decent person argue either side and mean it? If one
side is obviously right, it is not a contradiction, it is a lesson. Rewrite it.

**Keep the contradiction non-ethically-loaded.** The bind must not rest on a vulnerable
person's welfare. Do not make the competing party someone with a medical emergency, a sick
child, an elderly resident at heat risk, or anyone whose harm would make the "selfish" choice
feel cruel rather than merely costly. That contaminates the measure (participants answer to the
ethics, not to the framing) and puts them in a distressing spot. Use binds among equals
instead: a commons dilemma (your comfort vs a building-wide brownout that hits everyone), a
zero-sum split among ordinary neighbours (3C also wants cool for a birthday dinner), your
comfort tonight vs your own future self on a hotter day, or comfort vs a collective
sustainability target. The cost should be real and the heat genuinely unwanted, but the
opposing claim should be a peer's ordinary preference or a shared infrastructure risk, never
someone's safety.

## How the axes ride on the contradiction

The same contradiction should be legible across all four cells, but the cell decides *who
faces it and with what information*:
- Shared cells expose the other side of the contradiction (the neighbour's need, the building's
  stake). That is what lets the collective-legitimacy hypothesis show up.
- Private cells keep the contradiction internal (your comfort vs your future self), so there is
  no collective frame to lean on.
- High-agency cells let the participant act on the contradiction (allocate, share, adjust).
- Low-agency cells make ARKI resolve it and show the participant the result.

## Anti-solutionism checklist

Before approving a scenario, confirm:
- The technology does not dissolve the problem; it redistributes it.
- There is at least one sentence the participant could disagree with.
- The unfavourable outcome is concrete and personal, not a statistic.
- Nothing in the copy tells the participant how to feel about it.

## Believability without a wall of text

Plausibility should come from the interface state (a forecast, a building grid, a budget bar,
a neighbour's note), not from paragraphs of backstory. If you are explaining the world in
prose, you are over-writing it. Put the world on the screen and let the participant infer it.
